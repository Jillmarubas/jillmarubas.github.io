## CHAPTER 1 — THE CLOUD IS A BUILDING — 2:00 to 7:20

[T] Chapter 1: The Cloud Is a Building

[M] Calm, curious music.

We call it "the cloud."

It's one of the most successful words in the history of marketing. A cloud is soft. White. Floating. It weighs nothing.

But there is no cloud.

There are buildings. Concrete, steel, copper and glass. Thousands of them.

So let's follow your dinner question, step by step.

[V] Animated line: from a phone, under the street, along the ocean floor, into a building.

When you hit enter, your words become pulses of light. Those pulses race through fiber-optic cables under your street, along highways, sometimes across the ocean floor, until they reach a data center.

Inside that data center, your question lands on a very special kind of chip.

[V] Close-up macro shot of a GPU board. Slow rotation.

It's called a GPU, a graphics processing unit. Originally, these chips were built to draw video games. Explosions. Shadows. Water reflections.

Then researchers discovered they were also incredibly good at something else: doing millions of simple math problems at the same time.

And that is all modern AI really is underneath. Math. An unbelievable amount of math, done all at once.

One of the most popular AI chips in the world, Nvidia's H100, can draw up to 700 watts of power. [S4]

[T] 1 chip ≈ a small space heater, running nonstop

That's one chip.

A large AI data center can hold tens of thousands of them. The newest "AI factories" are being planned with hundreds of thousands.

[V] Slow walk down a server aisle. Blinking blue lights, endless racks.

Now, here's the part of physics nobody puts in the advertisement.

Almost every watt that goes into a chip comes out as heat.

Walk into the "hot aisle" of a data center and it hits you like opening an oven door. Warm air pushes against your face. The fans roar so loud that workers wear ear protection. The floor vibrates under your shoes.

[M] Sound design: fans, a deep electrical hum.

This is what "the cloud" sounds like.

[V] Aerial footage: Loudoun County, Virginia. Rows of identical grey buildings next to houses.

[T] Loudoun County, Virginia — "Data Center Alley"

If you want to see the cloud with your own eyes, go to Northern Virginia.

Loudoun County has more data center capacity than almost anywhere else on the planet. [S5] Grey boxes the size of shopping malls, right next to neighborhoods, schools and farmland.

There are two big jobs these buildings do for AI.

The first is **training.**

[V] Simple animation: a giant brain-shaped cloud of dots being "filled" with text.

Training is when a company feeds a model a huge part of the internet: books, articles, code, conversations. The machines run for weeks or months, adjusting billions of tiny settings until the model learns to predict the next word.

Think of training like building a factory. You do it once, and it's massively expensive.

The second job is **inference.**

Inference is what happens every time you ask a question. The factory is built. Now it's producing. One answer at a time.

And here's what changed everything.

For years, people worried mostly about training. But today, hundreds of millions of people use AI chatbots every single week. [S6] Students. Coders. Doctors. People asking what to make for dinner.

One answer is tiny.

Billions of answers are not.

[V] A counter on screen ticks up faster and faster.

And the questions are getting heavier.

A simple text answer is the lightest thing AI does. Ask it to make an image, and it works much harder. Ask it to make a video, harder still. And the newest "reasoning" models don't answer right away. They think first, sometimes for many seconds or minutes, running through possibilities before they reply. All that thinking is more math. More chips. More power.

[V] Construction footage: cranes, concrete, a flat Texas landscape.

[T] Stargate — announced January 2025 — up to $500 billion

That's why the buildings keep getting bigger.

In January 2025, OpenAI, SoftBank and Oracle announced a project called Stargate, with plans to spend up to 500 billion dollars on AI infrastructure in the United States. The first giant site rose out of the flat ground near Abilene, Texas. [S35]

Five hundred billion dollars. That's more than the yearly economy of many countries. Most of it goes to buildings, chips and power.

Every one of those answers needs a chip. Every chip needs power. And every watt of power turns into heat that has to go somewhere.

So these buildings need two things to survive.

Electricity. And a way to get rid of heat.

Let's start with electricity, because this is where the story gets strange.

## CHAPTER 3 — THE WATER — 13:30 to 19:00

[T] Chapter 3: The Water

[M] Soft, cold ambient tones. Dripping water.

Think about what your body does on a hot day.

You sweat. The sweat evaporates off your skin, and as it evaporates, it carries heat away. You cool down.

Many data centers cool themselves in exactly the same way.

[V] Animation: hot air from servers → cooling tower → water evaporating into the sky.

Hot air from the servers heats up water. That water flows into cooling towers, where some of it evaporates into the sky. The heat leaves with it.

Here's the important part.

That evaporated water doesn't flow back into the river. It doesn't go back to the town. It's gone, carried away as vapor. Scientists call this water *consumption*, not just water *use*.

And there's a second, hidden layer. The power plants that make electricity for data centers often use huge amounts of water for cooling too. So AI drinks twice: once at the data center, and once at the power plant.

[V] Return to The Dalles. Columbia River. Green hills. The Google facility in the distance.

Now, back to The Dalles.

Google has been in this town since the mid-2000s. Cheap hydropower from the Columbia River. Cool climate. A perfect place for a data center.

In 2021, the city was planning to give Google more water rights. A local newspaper wanted to know how much water Google was already using.

The city said that information was a trade secret. Google's secret.

So the newspaper sued.

[M] Tension builds slightly.

For more than a year, the case went back and forth. Then, in late 2022, the city settled and released the numbers. [S1]

[T] 2021: Google used ~355 million gallons in The Dalles

In 2021, Google's data centers in The Dalles used around 355 million gallons of water. That was more than a quarter of all the water used by the entire city. And it had nearly tripled in five years. [S1]

[V] Stock footage: a resident watering a garden. A farmer checking an irrigation line.

A quarter of a town's water. Going to one company. And until a newspaper went to court, the people who lived there weren't allowed to know.

Now, to be fair, Google pointed out it also paid for local water infrastructure, and that the area has a lot of water from the river system. [S1] The Dalles is not a desert.

But many data centers are being built in places that are.

[V] Map: arrows pointing to Arizona, Texas, northern Mexico, Chile, Spain.

Across the dry American Southwest, around Phoenix, in Texas, in parts of Spain and Chile, data centers are being planned and built in places already worried about drought.

[V] Footage: Montevideo, Uruguay, 2023. Empty reservoir. People carrying bottled water.

In 2023, Uruguay went through its worst drought in decades. In the capital, Montevideo, tap water turned salty because the reservoirs ran so low. People lined up for bottled water.

At the same time, Google was planning a data center there. Protesters took to the streets, angry that a tech company might get water while families were buying it in bottles. Google later said it would redesign the project to use a cooling system that needs far less water. [S13]

The same thing happened in Chile, where a court pushed back on Google's permit near Santiago, and Google again said it would switch to air cooling. [S13]

[V] Graphs rising on screen.

And this isn't just about one company.

In 2022, the same year AI started to explode, Microsoft's water consumption jumped 34%. Google's went up about 20%. Outside researchers said a big part of that was likely AI. [S14]

So how much water does ONE AI answer actually use?

[V] Split screen. Left: a 500ml water bottle. Right: a teaspoon with a few drops.

Here's where it gets confusing.

In 2023, researchers at the University of California, Riverside estimated that a conversation of roughly 10 to 50 questions with ChatGPT could "drink" about a 500 millilitre bottle of water. [S15]

But in 2025, OpenAI's CEO, Sam Altman, wrote that the average ChatGPT question uses about one-fifteenth of a teaspoon of water. [S16]

[T] A bottle per conversation... or a few drops per question?

A bottle of water. Or a few drops.

They can't both be right.

Can they?

Millions of them.

## CHAPTER 4 — THE HANDS — 19:00 to 26:00

[T] Chapter 4: The Hands

[M] Quiet piano. Slower pace.

To understand this chapter, you need to understand one uncomfortable fact.

When an AI model learns from the internet, it learns from ALL of the internet.

The encyclopedias and the poems. But also the hate. The violence. The abuse. The darkest corners that most of us never see.

[V] Blurred, abstract text scrolling, unreadable.

Early AI models could produce horrifying things. Racist rants. Violent threats. Descriptions of abuse. Not because anyone wanted them to, but because they learned it from us.

So AI companies had a problem. How do you teach a machine to recognize the worst content in the world, so it can refuse to repeat it?

You show it examples.

Thousands and thousands of examples, each one labeled by a human: *this is violence. This is abuse. This is hate.*

Someone has to read them first.

[V] A modern office building in Nairobi, Kenya. Morning traffic outside.

[T] Nairobi, Kenya — late 2021

In late 2021, OpenAI hired an outsourcing company called Sama for exactly this work. Sama was based in San Francisco but employed workers in Nairobi, Kenya. It marketed itself as an "ethical AI" company that helped lift people out of poverty. [S2]

In 2023, an investigation by TIME magazine revealed what that job looked like. [S2]

[V] Slow pan across an empty office. Rows of desks. Monitors glowing.

The workers were young Kenyans, many recent graduates. Their job was to read text passages pulled from the darkest parts of the internet and label them.

Descriptions of child sexual abuse. Murder. Suicide. Torture. Self-harm.

According to TIME, workers were expected to read and label roughly 150 to 250 passages in a nine-hour shift. [S2]

[M] Music drops out. Only room tone.

Sit with that for a second.

Nine hours. One screen. Passage after passage. You can't skip one. Skipping isn't doing your job. Your eyes start to ache. Your shoulders tighten. You go home, you sit down to eat with your family, and the words are still there behind your eyes.

One worker told TIME that reading one of those passages left him with recurring visions. He described the work in two words.

[T] "That was torture." — Sama worker, to TIME (2023)

[V] Text appears one figure at a time.

So what were they paid?

According to TIME, the workers took home between about $1.32 and $2 an hour, depending on their role. [S2]

[T] OpenAI → Sama: ~$12.50 per hour
[T] Sama → workers: ~$1.32 – $2 per hour

The contract said OpenAI paid Sama around $12.50 an hour for the work. [S2]

Workers said the counseling they were offered was rare and not enough. Sama disputed that, and said wellness support was available. [S2]

In February 2022, Sama ended the contract with OpenAI eight months early. [S2]

But the story didn't end when the contract did.

In 2023, a group of former workers in Kenya filed a petition asking the country's parliament to investigate. They wanted laws to protect people who do this kind of work, and they wanted the work recognized as a real health hazard, like working with dangerous chemicals. [S36]

Some of the workers who moderated content for big tech companies in Kenya later took those companies to court. Several said they had been diagnosed with post-traumatic stress disorder. [S36]

Researchers even have a name for this hidden workforce: "ghost work." Work that makes technology look automatic... because the humans doing it are kept out of sight.

OpenAI said that this kind of work was a necessary part of making AI safe, and that it had not set the productivity targets. [S2]

[V] Return to a chatbot screen. A polite, friendly answer types out.

Now here's the connection most people never make.

That polite, careful, friendly tone your AI uses? That's not natural. It's taught.

Part of it comes from a method called *reinforcement learning from human feedback.* In plain words: people read AI answers and rank them. *This answer is better. This one is worse. This one is harmful.* Do that millions of times, and the AI learns how to behave.

[V] Montage: laptops in small apartments, internet cafés, kitchen tables.

Every "please," every "I'm sorry, I can't help with that," every careful answer was shaped by human hands.

And today that's a giant industry.

There's a company called Scale AI, one of the biggest players in this world. In 2025, Meta invested over 14 billion dollars into it. [S17]

Behind companies like this are huge networks of workers, often in Kenya, the Philippines, India, Venezuela, and other places where wages are low.

In 2023, the Washington Post reported on workers in the Philippines doing AI labeling on a Scale AI platform called Remotasks. Many said they were paid far below the minimum wage, and that payments were often delayed or cancelled. [S18]

In 2024, nearly a hundred Kenyan data workers wrote an open letter to the President of the United States. They said their working conditions amounted to modern-day slavery. [S19]

[M] A slow, low tone.

To be fair, the picture is changing. Some AI companies now hire doctors, lawyers and scientists, and pay them very well, to train more advanced models. Some workers say this work changed their lives for the better.

But that creates a two-tier system. Experts on top, well paid. And underneath them, a hidden army doing the hardest, most traumatic work for a few dollars an hour.

[V] A single empty desk. The monitor switches off.

When we talk about the cost of AI, we usually talk about money. Billions in investment. Trillions in stock market value.

We rarely talk about a young graduate in Nairobi who can't sleep.

[V] Fade to a close-up of raw grey rock in a gloved hand.

But even the workers are only one link in the chain.

Because before there's a chatbot... before there's a data center... before there's a chip...

There's a rock in the ground.

## CHAPTER 5 — THE ROCKS — 26:00 to 30:00

[T] Chapter 5: The Rocks

[M] Deep, earthy percussion.

Every AI chip begins as sand.

Ordinary sand, rich in silicon. It's melted, purified until it's almost perfect, and grown into a gleaming crystal cylinder. That cylinder is sliced into thin wafers. [V] Clean-room footage: workers in white suits, yellow light, robotic arms.

[T] TSMC — Taiwan

The Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company, TSMC, makes the vast majority of the world's most advanced chips, including the ones inside most AI systems. [S20]

[V] Dry reservoir in Taiwan, 2021. Cracked mud.

Think about that. The world's AI depended, in part, on a fleet of water trucks driving across one island.

[V] Map: Taiwan, with China about 100 miles across the strait.

## CHAPTER 7 — THE OTHER SIDE — 35:00 to 38:00

## How the skill was applied
- **Hook:** opens in the middle of the action with something that doesn't add up: a "weightless" answer, then a town suing Google over water.
- **Viewer arc:** the false belief at the start is "AI lives in a weightless cloud"; the truth the viewer reaches by the end is "AI is physical, and someone pays." This is a positive change arc.
- **Bonding with real people:** Nairobi workers, Memphis residents, The Dalles, a family in Ohio. All are real, documented situations. No invented characters.
- **Show, don't tell:** the narration doesn't describe what the visuals already show. Emotions are written as physical sensations (heat from the hot aisle, aching eyes, the hum you feel in your chest).
- **Rhythm:** short, punchy lines at tense moments (Three Mile Island, Nairobi, the ending). Longer explaining lines in between, and calmer music after each high point.
- **Fairness:** each chapter includes the company's side. Chapter 7 is entirely the counterargument. This builds trust and avoids one-sided "doom" content.

## B-roll suggestions
- Free or cheap stock: data center aisles, server racks, cooling towers, power lines, clean rooms, Taiwan reservoirs, steam engine engravings.
- Archive: Three Mile Island 1979 news footage. Check the license before using.
- Make yourself: maps, counters, split screens, stat cards (Canva, After Effects, or Remotion).
- Always show the source on screen when you cite a number. Put a screenshot of the headline or report cover in the corner.
