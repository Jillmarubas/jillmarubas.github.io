# Script: "Why Your Laptop Gets Slower Every Year (It's Not Age)"

Written with the user's guide "How to Write a Million-View Explainer Script — Step by Step" (Claude Docs) and the
plan in `SCRIPT_PLAN_laptop.md`.

**Format (user, 2 Nov 2026):** the AI avatar appears only in the thumbnail. The video itself is motion graphics,
typography, 2.5D/3D objects and visual effects: no presenter, no drawn people. So the guide's recurring pair is two
objects: **DAY ONE** (the laptop new, glossy, instant) and **TODAY** (the same laptop, dusty, slow). The voice still
speaks to "you", so the body lines stay in the narration while the picture shows objects. House look: the MacBook
style (16:9, 60 fps, dark, After Effects-style motion) per the motion-video SOP.

Each `[S..]` cue gives: the scene, the objects and what they do, on-screen text (keywords and numbers only), and one
sound. Length: 741 words of narration, about 5:50 with pauses at 145 words a minute. `[VERIFY]` marks the one figure still to confirm before recording. Pauses are written "…".

## Hook (0:00–0:50)

**[S01]** Black void. One 3D laptop, lid closed, lit from above. Small typography top left: "9:02". Sound: a single click.
> It's nine-oh-two. You press the power button.

**[S02]** Push in as the lid opens. Through the vents, fan blades glow and spin up. The screen stays black. Sound: fan whir rising.
> The fan wakes up first. Then… nothing.

**[S03]** Macro on the screen: a huge loading spinner turning. Sound: a soft tick on every turn.
> A little circle spins. You tap the desk. Your jaw goes tight.

**[S04]** Whip pan into a split screen. Left, "DAY ONE": the same laptop, glossy, protective film still on, screen lit instantly. Right, "TODAY": dust specks, dimmer glow, the spinner. Sound: a bright chime on the left.
> Now go back to day one. You opened the lid, and it was ready before your coffee was.

**[S05]** Both halves freeze. A thin line draws between the two chips glowing inside them. Text: "SAME CHIP".
> Same laptop. Same chip.
> So why was it instant then… and torture now?

**[S06]** Two statements slam in as big typography, then crack: "IT WEARS OUT" · "THEY SLOW IT ON PURPOSE".
> You've heard the answers. Computers wear out. Or the company slows them down on purpose.
> One of those turned out to be half true. And the real reason is stranger.

**[S07]** A big "3" turns into three empty slots: CLUE 1 · CLUE 2 · CLUE 3. The camera dives toward the keyboard.
> There are three things slowing your laptop down. The first one is hiding under your keyboard.

## Clue 1: the heat (0:50–2:15)

**[S08]** Exploded 3D view: the keyboard lifts away to show the chip, a copper heat pipe and the fan. Labels: CHIP · PASTE · COOLER.
> Under the keys sits the chip. On top of it, a thin grey layer of paste. Its only job is to carry heat from the chip to the cooler.

**[S09]** Macro cross-section of the paste between chip and cooler. Each heat pulse glows orange, then fades blue; at every pulse, a bead of paste squeezes out at the edges. Text: "HOT → COLD".
> In 2000, engineers at Intel described what happens to that paste over time.
> Every time the chip heats up, everything expands. Every time it cools, it shrinks.
> And every cycle squeezes a little paste out of the gap. They called it pump-out.

**[S10]** The fan intake: dust particles drift in on the airflow and clump on the cooling fins. The airflow lines thin out. Sound: the fan, slightly muffled.
> Meanwhile, dust slips in with every breath of the fan. And it settles right where the heat is supposed to leave.

**[S11]** A temperature gauge climbs toward red. Text: "? °C". Hold for the pause.
> So the heat has nowhere to go. Here's your guess. How hot does a laptop chip let itself get before it fights back?
> … About a hundred degrees Celsius. Hot enough to boil water. **[VERIFY: the maximum chip temperature from Intel's or AMD's spec pages; if unconfirmed, cut this line and keep "Hot enough that it has to fight back."]**

**[S12]** As the gauge hits red, a clock-speed dial beside it drops from high to low. Text: "SLOWS DOWN". Sound: a low downward sweep.
> And when it gets there, the chip does the only thing it can.
> It slows itself down. On purpose. To survive.

**[S13]** Back to the TODAY laptop, fan roaring, a slight shake in the frame.
> So that roaring fan isn't your laptop dying. It's your laptop protecting itself.

**[S14]** A snowflake drifts across the cold laptop. Text: "COLD MORNING". A generic smartphone (no logo) slides in beside it.
> But heat can't explain why it's slow on a cold morning.
> The second clue starts with a phone… and a man who counted.

## Clue 2: the tired battery (2:15–3:50)

**[S15]** The smartphone on a dark plinth. Date typography: "DECEMBER 2017".
> December 2017. John Poole, the creator of the benchmark app Geekbench, was looking at speed scores sent in by iPhone owners.

**[S16]** Data visualisation: dots fall into one tall peak labelled "iOS 10.2.0", then, after a wipe to "iOS 10.2.1", part of the pile splits off into lower peaks.
> On one version of iOS, the phones scored about the same.
> After the next update, some of them suddenly scored lower. Same phones. Same chips.

**[S17]** Inside the phone, its battery icon ages: the bar dims, hairline cracks appear. Text: "OLD BATTERY".
> What did the slow ones have in common? Old batteries.

**[S18]** Typography: "APPLE CONFIRMED" and, smaller, "2 DAYS LATER". The phone's screen flickers off for a frame, then a speed dial beside it lowers.
> Two days later, Apple confirmed it.
> An old battery couldn't always deliver peak power, and the phone could shut down without warning. So the software slowed the phone instead.

**[S19]** A price tag flips from "$79" to "$29". Then a counter starts spinning upward.
> Apple apologised, and cut the price of a new battery from seventy-nine dollars to twenty-nine.
> So here's your guess. How many people took that offer? A million? Two?

**[S20]** The counter lands, huge: "11,000,000+". Hold one beat. Sound: a deep hit on the landing.
> … Over eleven million.

**[S21]** A recreated legal page (plain typography, no logos), stamped: "SETTLEMENT · UP TO $500M".
> Later, Apple agreed to pay up to five hundred million dollars to settle the lawsuits.

**[S22]** A fresh battery slides into the phone. The speed dial swings back to full. Text: "FULL SPEED".
> And here's the part that matters for you. A new battery gave those phones their full speed back.
> The chip was never the problem.

**[S23]** Back to the laptop. A generic battery-health readout appears above it: "BATTERY HEALTH: ?".
> That was phones. Laptops manage power their own way, so nobody is saying yours does exactly this.
> But a tired battery is worth checking before you blame the chip.

**[S24]** Typography "1995" in an old terminal font. A box labelled "SOFTWARE" lands on the laptop with a thud.
> Heat and batteries are the small part. The biggest weight was predicted in 1995… by a man who built programming languages.

## Clue 3: the heavier software (3:50–4:40)

**[S25]** A recreated magazine page (typography only): "COMPUTER · FEBRUARY 1995", headline "A Plea for Lean Software".
> His name was Niklaus Wirth. He created the Pascal programming language. And in 1995 he wrote a warning.

**[S26]** The quote reveals word by word, large, centred. Sound: quiet; a single low tone under it.
> "Software is getting slower more rapidly than hardware becomes faster."

**[S27]** A short 3D stack of memory blocks. Label: "WINDOWS XP · 2001 · 64 MB".
> Here's what he meant. In 2001, Windows XP needed sixty-four megabytes of memory to run.

**[S28]** A second, empty stack beside it: "WINDOWS 11 · ?".
> Windows 11 needs… Your guess. Twice as much? Ten times?

**[S29]** The second stack grows to 64 times the first; the camera tilts up to follow it. Text: "4 GB · ×64". Sound: a rising whoosh-free swell, then a hit.
> … Sixty-four times as much. Just to start.

**[S30]** Plain notification cards (no brand names) rain down: "Update available". Each drops a small block onto the laptop.
> And it doesn't stop there. Your browser updates. Your apps update.
> Each one adds a feature, a little animation, one more thing running in the background.

**[S31]** The TODAY laptop now carries a tower of blocks taller than itself. The hinge creaks.
> None of them asked if your laptop could carry it.
> Your laptop didn't get weaker. The work got heavier.

## Payoff (4:40–5:35)

**[S32]** Return to the S04 split: DAY ONE and TODAY.
> So let's go back to the question from the start.
> Same laptop. Same chip. Why was it instant then… and torture now?

**[S33]** The three clue slots from S07 fill one by one: "PASTE" · "BATTERY" · "×64".
> The paste slowly moved out. The battery got tired. And the software got sixty-four times hungrier.

**[S34]** DAY ONE carries one small block; TODAY carries the tower. Inside both, the chip glows exactly the same.
> On day one, your laptop carried a light bag. Today it carries everything every update ever added.
> Same strength. Heavier load.

**[S35]** A checklist in large type, ticking: "BATTERY HEALTH" · "CLEAN VENTS" · "STARTUP APPS".
> So before you buy a new one, try three things.
> Check your battery's health. Have the vents cleaned. Turn off the apps that start with your laptop.
> And if the battery ever looks swollen, don't open it yourself. Take it to a repair shop.

**[S36]** The tower of blocks lifts away. The TODAY laptop's glow warms to match DAY ONE. The line appears in large type.
> Because your laptop isn't old. It's carrying more.

## Ending (5:35–5:50)

**[S37]** Slow pull-back: the laptop becomes small in a vast dark space, with faint blocks drifting toward it from every side.
> Maybe the real question isn't how long your laptop lasts.
> It's who decides what it has to carry.

**[S38]** End card: a "Send" button pressed; a thin line of light races across a globe and back. The question appears in type.
> Next: what really happens in the two seconds after you press Send on ChatGPT?
