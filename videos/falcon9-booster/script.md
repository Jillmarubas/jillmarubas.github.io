Full booster in a clean white studio with a soft floor shadow. *(Studio illustration: crews don't stand on top of a real booster.)*

That's a person.

Fourteen storeys tall. It falls out of the sky… and lands standing up, on a target in the middle of the ocean.

Here's the crazy part.
**It can't hover.**

It gets one shot to hit zero speed at exactly zero height. Too early, it flies back up. Too late… it crashes.
So how does it do the maths?
To answer that, we have to take it apart.

## 1. THE GIANT, TO SCALE (0:35–1:25)

**VISUAL:** Full Falcon 9 stack (booster + second stage + fairing) next to a bus, a person and a house. Dimension lines draw on.

**VO:**
Here's the whole Falcon 9: seventy metres tall, just three-point-six-six metres wide. Long and skinny, like a pencil.

**VISUAL:** Second stage and fairing lift off the top and drift away. The booster stays.

**VO:**
Today we're opening the bottom part: the first stage, the booster.
It does the heavy lifting for the first couple of minutes… then it turns around and comes home.

**VISUAL:** Booster glows faintly: four grid fins near the top, four folded legs at the base, nine engines underneath. Quick labels pop on each.

**VO:**
Grid fins near the top. Landing legs at the bottom. Nine engines underneath.
Every one of these matters for the landing. But first… what fills the other forty metres?

---

## 2. THE TANKS (1:25–2:45)

**VISUAL:** Cutaway slices the booster lengthwise. Two tanks: liquid oxygen on top (cold blue), kerosene below (amber). A shared dome between them.

**VO:**
Almost the whole booster is fuel tank. Two of them.
On top: liquid oxygen, chilled so cold it's a liquid. Below it: RP-1, a highly refined kerosene.
They share a single curved wall, a common dome, which saves weight.

**VISUAL:** Mass counters fill: **LOX ≈ 287 t**, **RP-1 ≈ 124 t**.

**VO:**
Together, around four hundred tonnes of propellant. More than nine-tenths of the booster's weight on the pad is stuff it's going to burn.

**VISUAL:** A pipe lights up running down through the centre of the kerosene tank.

**VO:**
But here's a puzzle. The oxygen is on top, and the engines are at the bottom. So how does it get past the kerosene?
Straight through it. A double-walled tube runs right down the middle of the fuel tank.

**VISUAL:** Helium bottles light up; arrows push down on both liquids as they drain.

**VO:**
And as the tanks empty, something has to fill the space, or the walls would buckle like a crushed can. That's heated helium, pushing down on the propellant and holding the tanks firm.
The walls themselves? Aluminium-lithium alloy, friction-stir welded.
Remember how much fuel is in here, because by the time this booster tries to land… almost all of it will be gone.

---

## 3. ONE MERLIN ENGINE (2:45–4:45)

**VISUAL:** Camera flies down the side of the booster to the engines and pulls one Merlin out. It explodes into parts with labels: turbopump, gas generator, injector, combustion chamber, nozzle.

**VO:**
This is a Merlin. Nine of these push the booster off the ground.
And the hard part isn't burning fuel. It's *moving* it.

**VISUAL:** Turbopump cutaway spins up. Blue oxygen and amber kerosene stream in.

**VO:**
A turbopump drags oxygen and kerosene in, enormous amounts every second, and rams them into the engine at high pressure.
What spins the pump? A small burner of its own: the gas generator. It burns a little fuel just to power the pump, then dumps the exhaust out the side.

**VISUAL:** Injector closeup: a single central pintle sprays fuel into a ring of oxygen. They mix into a flame front.

**VO:**
The fuel and oxygen meet at the injector. The Merlin uses a pintle design, a single central post that sprays them together, a cousin of the engine that landed Apollo astronauts on the Moon.
It burns stable even when you turn it way down.
Hold on to that. It's about to matter.

**VISUAL:** Flame fills the combustion chamber; exhaust accelerates out the bell. A thrust arrow grows: **854 kN**.

**VO:**
The hot gas blasts out the nozzle, and the rocket gets pushed the other way.
One Merlin: up to eight hundred and fifty-four kilonewtons. That's like lifting eighty-seven tonnes.

**VISUAL:** Throttle dial turns down. The arrow shrinks to **482 kN**, then a stop marker appears: **MINIMUM**.

**VO:**
And it can throttle down, but only so far. Its lowest setting is about four hundred and eighty-two kilonewtons. Not zero.
Remember that number. It's the reason this rocket can't hover.

## 4. NINE AS ONE (4:45–5:45)

**VISUAL:** Engine slides back into place. Camera looks straight up into the base: eight engines in a ring around one in the centre.

**VO:**
Nine engines: eight in a circle, one in the middle. Together, over seven and a half thousand kilonewtons at liftoff.

**VISUAL:** Engines gimbal; tiny vector arrows tilt and the booster's path bends.

**VO:**
There are no wings or fins to steer on the way up. Instead, the engines themselves swivel a few degrees, pushing the rocket's tail sideways to point the nose.

**VISUAL:** One engine goes dark; the others brighten slightly and the flight path holds.

**VO:**
And with nine engines, it can lose one and often still make it to orbit.
But going up is the easy part.
Coming down, this booster will use only one of them.

## 5. LETTING GO (5:45–6:35)

**VISUAL:** Launch montage in the white-studio style: liftoff, sky darkens to black. Engines cut off. Latches at the interstage light up.

**VO:**
About two and a half minutes after liftoff, the engines shut down.
High-pressure helium pops three latches, and pneumatic pushers shove the two stages apart.

**VISUAL:** Second stage ignites and leaves; the booster drifts, alone, high above Earth.

**VO:**
The second stage carries on to orbit.
And the booster… is now a fourteen-storey empty can, coasting at around six times the speed of sound, with almost no fuel left.
It has about five minutes to get home.

---

## 6. THE WAY HOME (6:35–8:25)

**VISUAL:** Small nitrogen thrusters puff at the top. The booster slowly flips end over end.

**VO:**
First, it has to turn around. Little nitrogen thrusters near the top puff gas and flip the booster end over end.

**VISUAL:** Three engines light: the **BOOSTBACK** burn. The trajectory line bends back toward a landing target.

**VO:**
Then three engines relight to kill its forward speed, and send it back toward the landing site. That's the boostback burn.

**VISUAL:** Grid fins unfold and lock: titanium, waffle-shaped. Airflow lines through them.

**VO:**
Then these unfold: the grid fins. Single pieces of titanium, shaped like a waffle. As air rushes through them, they tilt and steer the booster like a skydiver's hands.

**VISUAL:** The booster falls back into the atmosphere; heat glow on the engine end. **ENTRY BURN**: three engines fire into the oncoming air (~70 km → ~40 km on screen).

**VO:**
Now it's falling back into thicker air, engines first, very fast. To keep from tearing itself apart, it fires three engines again *into* the wind: the entry burn. A shield of exhaust and a sharp slowdown.

**VISUAL:** "But" turn: the screen cools and desaturates. A 2018 booster's fins freeze; it starts to spin and lands in the sea short of the pad.

**VO:**
But this only works if every part works.
In 2018, a hydraulic pump that moves the grid fins stalled. The fins froze… and the booster spun into the ocean, just off the coast.

**VISUAL:** Back to clean light. Our booster glides down, fins making tiny corrections; altitude counter ticking down.

**VO:**
Ours is fine. It's gliding now, steering with its fins, falling at hundreds of metres per second toward a target a few dozen metres wide.
And now it has to stop.
Using an engine… that can't go low enough to hover.

## 7. THE MATH OF LANDING (8:25–10:25)

**VISUAL:** Clean graphic split: the booster on the right; a balance bar on the left. **THRUST ↑ 482 kN** vs **WEIGHT ↓ ~250 kN**.

**VO:**
Here's the problem.
Remember the Merlin's lowest setting? Four hundred and eighty-two kilonewtons.
But an almost-empty booster weighs only around two hundred and fifty.
Even one engine, turned all the way down, pushes nearly twice as hard as gravity pulls.

**VISUAL:** Ghost booster: thrust tips the balance and it starts climbing back up. Red ✕.

**VO:**
So if it slowed to a stop in mid-air… it would start going back *up*.
It can't hold still. It has to hit zero speed at exactly zero height. Engineers call it a hoverslam.

**VISUAL:** Formula types on, one symbol at a time, as each is spoken: **v² = 2·a·d**. Each symbol links to the booster: v = speed arrow, a = braking arrow, d = height bar.

**VO:**
The maths is one line from school physics.
Speed squared equals two, times the braking, times the distance.
Let's plug in an example.

**VISUAL:** Values fill in: **v = 250 m/s**, **a = 2 g ≈ 19.6 m/s²** → **d ≈ 1,600 m**. Labelled **EXAMPLE**.

**VO:**
Say it's falling at two hundred and fifty metres per second, and braking at twice the force of gravity. It needs about one-point-six kilometres to stop.
So the computer lights the engine at almost exactly that height. Not a second early. Not a second late.

**VISUAL:** Booster falls with live **SPEED** and **ALTITUDE** counters. Engine lights; speed drains as altitude drains. Two bars race to zero together.

**VO:**
And here's the twist: as it burns, it gets lighter. Same thrust, less mass, so it brakes *harder* every second.
So the computer re-runs this maths over and over, many times a second, nudging the throttle so both numbers reach zero together.

**VISUAL:** Legs swing down and lock. Final metres: speed under 10, then 5, then 0.

**VO:**
In the last seconds, the legs swing down and lock…
and both numbers hit zero, together.

**VISUAL:** Touchdown. Silence for a beat. Steam. A single click as the engine cuts.

**VO:**
*(silence, ~1.5 s)*

**VISUAL:** "But" turn, quick: 2024, a leg collapses and the booster tips off the droneship. Cut back to a landed booster, with a counter: **B1067 · 37 flights**.

**VO:**
It doesn't always work. In 2024, a leg collapsed on touchdown and the booster toppled into the sea.
But one booster, B1067, has now done this thirty-seven times.

## Numbers locked (23 Oct 2026)
- "Faster than a bullet" is cut: no published figure for the booster's speed just before the landing burn.
- Speed at separation: "around six times the speed of sound" (Mach ~6 for a booster that will land, per Elon Musk as cited in a NASASpaceflight thread; webcast overlays read 6,000–8,000 km/h). Secondary source, so kept as "around".
- Empty weight "around 250 kN": empty mass ≈ 22.2 t (Wikipedia, Falcon 9 Block 5, cited) plus a few tonnes of leftover propellant, × 9.81. Stays "around".
- Tank masses: Wikipedia, Falcon 9 Block 5 (cited); the VO says only "around four hundred tonnes".
